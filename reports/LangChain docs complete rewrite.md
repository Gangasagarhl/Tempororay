# Archive everything, rewrite free, distrust the reviewer

An offline lab can hold every published word of the LangChain docs, but `llms-full.txt` alone falls well short: it omits **502 live pages (4.64 MB, mostly integrations)** and reduces **720 API-endpoint pages to one-line stubs**. Fill those two holes from the per-page `.md` URLs, then archive the `langchain-ai/docs` repo, the API reference and the old versions from Git. A $0 rewrite is feasible on **Google AI Studio's Gemini Flash-Lite free tier: about one day of writing and 3 to 5 daily quota windows with review**, but those limits come from a third-party table because Google does not publish them, so the signed-in console must confirm them first. Without Gemini, writing alone takes 3 to 6 days across several providers, or 5 to 12 days of nonstop running on this laptop. **OpenCode's free models should not carry this job**: updating from 1.19.3 to v2 is worth trying, yet the same limit error persists on v2.2.6, no cap is published for the main free models, and they cannot be called from a script. Automatic checking is reliable for code, names, numbers, links and structure, because parsers can compare those exactly. It is not reliable for "every fact": the best model-based omission check in the strongest study caught only **25% to 37%** of lost content. The pipeline therefore needs a fallback to the original sentence and a seeded-error test, and the course should claim only what those checks actually prove.

## The offline copy lacks 502 published pages, and the sitemap will not reveal them

`llms-full.txt` is a faithful copy of what it includes. A line diff of 20 sampled pages found the 19 prose pages differ from their `.md` URLs only by a banner, a blockquoted description and absolute links ([agents.md](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/agents.md)). It also keeps Python and JavaScript as separate pages rather than dropping one: 192 Python and 199 JavaScript OSS sections, with 182 paths in both ([llms-full.txt](https://docs.langchain.com/llms-full.txt)).

The losses are elsewhere. Of 1,358 page sources in the repo, 546 have no URL in the sitemap or `llms-full.txt`. Requesting their 587 candidate `.md` URLs returned **502 live pages (348 Python, 149 JavaScript, 5 other)**, 71 redirects and 12 not-found responses, and 2 timed out and still need a retry ([repo](https://github.com/langchain-ai/docs), [sitemap](https://docs.langchain.com/sitemap.xml)). These pages include [ChatOpenAI](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/integrations/chat/openai.md) at 81,513 bytes and [ChatAnthropic](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/integrations/chat/anthropic.md) at 119,459 bytes. They add about 27% to the text and outnumber the 391 OSS pages `llms-full.txt` does hold. No official statement explains the exclusion, so the page list must come from the repo's `src/` tree, not from the site's own indexes.

The second hole is narrower but severe per page. 720 of the 1,727 sections are API stubs: `search-threads` is 249 characters in `llms-full.txt` and 9,171 in its [.md version](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/agent-server-api/threads/search-threads.md). The lab's existing 1,726 files in `source/pages` total about 20 MB, which suggests they already hold the expanded versions, but nobody has audited that. The saved `llms-full.txt` is also 12,231 bytes behind the live file, so the build should pin one repo commit and one fetch date.

## Gemini Flash-Lite writes it in a day; review pushes the job to 3 to 5

The capacity estimate assumes about 3M words: 4.0M input tokens and 4.0M to 5.0M output tokens. The notes do not say whether that includes the 502 missing pages. If it was sized on `llms-full.txt` alone, this report's arithmetic puts the upper bound near 5.1M input and 6.4M output, so count tokens on the final page set before scheduling anything. Fenced code never goes to the model, and Python and JavaScript twins share much of their prose, so a block-level hash cache that rewrites identical text once will pull the real figure down by an unmeasured amount.

Only one free tier approaches this volume in a day. A third-party table gives Gemini 3.5 and 3.1 Flash-Lite **10 requests per minute, 250K input tokens per minute and 500 requests per day each**, per project ([Scriptbyai](https://www.scriptbyai.com/gemini-api-free-tier-limits/)). Another listing claims 1,500 ([freellm.net](https://freellm.net/models/google-gemini/gemini-3-1-flash-lite)), and Google's public pages show no free-tier numbers at all ([AI Studio limits](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/rate-limits)). The pages read also do not state whether Google requires a card, so sign-up must confirm the no-card condition. Maximum output per Flash-Lite request was not read either, and the 8K chunk plan depends on it.

The one-account-each total is about **5.36M output per day at 4K chunks and 9.85M at 8K**, or 4.29M and 7.88M after a 20% retry loss. Remove Gemini and it falls to **0.88M to 1.53M per day, a 2.6 to 5.7 day job**, with uneven style from mixing models. Nobody has tested real throughput or error rates on any of these providers. Cohere's 1,000 monthly calls are a possible extra, but its per-call maximum was not read, and it describes these as evaluation keys ([Cohere](https://docs.cohere.com/docs/rate-limits)).

The published estimates count writing only, and review costs more tokens than writing. Two reviewer passes that each read the source and the rewrite consume about 17M input tokens (this report's arithmetic), which would take about 17 days on SambaNova's combined 1M tokens per day. Request-capped Gemini absorbs it far better. Writing on 3.5 Flash-Lite and reviewing on 3.1 Flash-Lite takes about 2 windows for writing and 3 for review at 8K chunks, overlapping, and 3 and 4 to 5 windows at 4K chunks. That gives **3 to 5 quota windows in total**, and windows reset at midnight Pacific. The two models come from one family, so a sample should also go to a non-Gemini reviewer. Independence between writer and reviewer is reasoned from principle; no study measured it for rewriting.

The local fallback is real but slow. This PC, read directly from Windows hardware information, has an i5-1235U and a single 16 GB DDR4-3200 module. Halving a [published dual-channel estimate](https://insiderllm.com/guides/cpu-only-llms-what-actually-works/) gives 5 to 9 tokens per second on a 3B to 4B model, or **5 to 12 days nonstop** plus an assumed 11 to 37 hours to read the input. A few minutes of `llama-bench` would replace that estimate with a measurement.

Terms favour a single account per provider and staying within quota. OpenRouter bans multiple accounts used to bypass limits ([terms](https://openrouter.ai/terms)). NVIDIA's trial covers "internal testing and evaluation", which is why it is left out ([terms](https://assets.ngc.nvidia.com/products/api-catalog/legal/NVIDIA%20API%20Trial%20Terms%20of%20Service.pdf)). Google may train on free-tier input and let human reviewers read it, which is acceptable for public docs, and its terms limit EEA, Swiss and UK client-facing use to paid services ([Gemini terms](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/terms)).

## OpenCode's free models cannot carry the rewrite, even after the v2 update

Update anyway, and treat it as a test. The pinned maintainer thread says v1 "no longer supports free models" and that updating "has fixed this for many people", but reports of the same error continue on v2.1.4 and v2.2.6 through 2026-11-09 ([#59387](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/issues/59387)). A near-identical 1.19.4 case recorded one 429 with a retry window of 8.95 hours and was closed as not planned ([#58205](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/issues/58205)). Another user hit the block after about 5 minutes on one prompt ([#58963](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/issues/58963)), and one report showed no reset for over 2 days ([#58814](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/issues/58814)).

Three facts rule it out for scheduled bulk work, whatever the update does. GLM-5.3-Flash Free and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash Free have no published cap and are free only "for a limited time". LongCat 2.5 Free has a stated cap of 100 requests per day, or 10 to 12.5 days at an optimistic 4K per request. And "free-tier chat models require OpenCode", so direct API calls fail with `FreeTierError` ([Zen docs](https://opencode.ai/docs/zen/), [#58205](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/issues/58205)). The attraction is privacy, since the two named models carry a zero-retention, no-training statement, but that does not help on public docs. No release note through v2.2.6 mentions free usage limits or retry behaviour ([releases](https://github.com/anomalyco/opencode/releases)), so any `opencode run` job needs the existing process-tree kill and log check regardless of version.

## Parsers prove names and numbers survived; models catch a third of lost facts

The failure this pipeline must catch is usually subtle. In GPT-4 medical simplifications, **74.1% of information loss was oversimplification and only 25.9% was outright deletion**, so a sentence can survive while losing its qualifier ([Trienes et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16475v2)). Across LLM simplifications of scientific abstracts, loss of informative content appeared in 19.56% of annotations and content unsupported by the source in 13.54% ([Vendeville et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2505.16392)). Models also shift expressed certainty in up to 75% of meaning-preserving rewrites, and most are 1.5 to 2 times as likely to raise it as to lower it ([Belem et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07951)). In docs, the matching risk is drift from "may" toward "must", although the abstract does not say whether modal verbs were analysed. None of this research covers software documentation, so its transfer here is an inference.

Soundness should set the order of the checks. External sound verifiers improve LLM output, while LLM self-critique causes "significant performance collapse" ([Stechly et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08115)). The evidence on model judges explains why they come last. In a 495-pair clinical study, judges separated added or changed content at 0.79 to 0.94 but omissions at only 0.50 to 0.63, where 0.5 is chance. Listing the source facts first and asking a closed present-or-absent question for each one lifted single-note omission detection to **24.6% at 2.7% false alarms, or 36.9% at 6.2%** ([Fox et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.31016v1)). Reviewer choice matters as much as prompt design: sentence-level faithfulness ranged from 48.1% for Phi-2 to 90.8% for Gemma2-27B-Instruct and 92.0% for Llama3-70B-Instruct ([FineSurE](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.00908v3)).

Off-the-shelf local checks are weaker still. Measures that work for summaries transfer badly: QAFactEval reached only 0.395 correlation with human comprehension on simplification ([Agrawal and Carpuat](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10126v1)), and the most SummaC-precise system was ranked last by humans on faithfulness ([Cripwell et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.03278)). [`nli-deberta-v3-small`](https://huggingface.co/cross-encoder/nli-deberta-v3-small) fits this PC easily, but it is a flag at best. MiniCheck-FT5, a 770M model, scored 74.7 balanced accuracy against GPT-4's 75.3 and T5-NLI's 61.0, and splitting claims into atomic facts did not help it ([Tang et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.10774v2)). CPU speed for these models is unmeasured, and MiniCheck's weights licence is unconfirmed.

Protection comes before checking. Parse each page with a real Markdown parser and send the model prose only. Regex fence detection broke placeholder sync in a production Markdown translator, which was fixed with `markdown-it-py`, atomic code blocks and list grouping ([Microsoft](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azuredevcommunityblog/fixing-broken-markdown-in-ai-translation-hardening-a-production-pipeline/4511378)). The `.md` pages are the simpler input, because they have MDX resolved. `remark-mdx` is available if the lab rewrites from raw repo sources ([MDX docs](https://mdxjs.com/packages/remark-mdx/)). Splice prose back by parser offsets so everything else stays byte-identical. Leave headings unchanged so anchors hold, and treat the flattened `> [tab]` and `> [card]` markers as protected structure.

| Check | What it proves | On failure |
|---|---|---|
| Block sequence, heading depth, list and table shape; code byte-identical | Structure and code intact | Hard fail: retry from source naming the defect, two tries at most |
| Placeholder count per block, as a multiset | No token lost or invented | Hard fail, same retry |
| Identifier set equality (inline code, CamelCase, snake_case, dotted paths, flags, env vars) | Names present, none invented | Hard fail, same retry |
| Number multiset after normalising ("two" = 2) | Numbers present; not that each attaches to the right thing | Hard fail, same retry |
| URL and anchor equality | Links intact | Hard fail, same retry |
| Negation, obligation and condition word classes per aligned sentence | Flags "must" drifting to "should" ([RFC 2119](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc2119)) or a lost "unless" | Flag: one retry naming the class, then keep the original |
| Sentence alignment, content-word coverage, two-way NLI (local) | Omission and addition suspects | Flag only: human queue, no automatic retry |
| Second-model coverage pass: list the facts, then present or absent per fact | Catches some lost facts and wrong bindings | One retry naming the fact, then restore the original sentence or flag |
| Second-model faithfulness pass, sentence by sentence, JSON with reason | Additions and changes, its strong suit | Flag: human queue with source and rewrite side by side |

Every retry should restart from the source, because certainty drift compounds across repeated paraphrasing: 20% to 40% over five rounds in one model and domain ([Belem et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.07951)). When retries run out, keep the original sentence and show a small "original wording kept" note. Identifier lists catch one failure placeholders cannot, namely a model that describes a class in words and drops the name. To make omissions structural, this report proposes having the model return each rewritten sentence tagged with its numbered source sentence, so an unmapped source ID fails mechanically while one-to-many splits still pass. That design is untested, as are the per-chunk placeholder legend and the quote-required coverage answers in the notes.

Readability scores should serve only as a trend. They must run on masked prose, because identifiers distort them in both directions: long names raise the score, and dots inside names create false sentence breaks ([textstat](https://github.com/textstat/textstat)). A rewrite can also score better simply by dropping content, so a readability gain counts only after the faithfulness checks pass.

No published catch rates exist for this domain, so the lab must generate its own. Take correct rewrites and mutate them: delete a sentence, change "must" to "may", drop a "not", alter a number, rename an identifier, remove an "unless" clause, add an outside fact. Report the catch rate per check. The same test decides whether a free Flash-Lite reviewer earns its place, since small and base models scored at chance in FineSurE.

## Conclusion

The expensive part of this project is not generating 4.5M tokens of simpler English; it is knowing which of them to trust. Writing fits in a free day, while verification takes several, and it exposes the difference between what can be proven and what can only be sampled. An honest course label follows from that split: code, identifiers, numbers and links machine-verified on every chunk, prose meaning reviewed at a measured catch rate, and unsimplified passages marked. The fallback rate then becomes the most useful quality number in the project, because it shows directly how much plain English the free model actually delivered safely.

Two checks settle the plan before any bulk call. The signed-in AI Studio page either confirms the Gemini quota and no-card access or sends the job to a 3 to 6 day mixed-provider route. And a repo-derived page list, pinned to one commit, defines "complete" more firmly than any index LangChain publishes, since its sitemap and `llms-full.txt` both leave out the 502 pages and `llms.txt` reports a slightly different total. The project's current reliance on OpenCode's free models for building should end at the scheduling stage, leaving OpenCode as an occasional interactive tool.
